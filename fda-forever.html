Forever Doesn't Allow

The therapy exists.
You are running out of time to reach it.

You have a diagnosis and a number of months. A therapy that might help is real — it is in a trial a flight away, or waiting behind a company that has not returned your doctor's call. Every click below spends time. Time is the only thing here that cannot be recovered.

This is a short, text-only game. There is no winning ending. We checked, and so did the people it is about.

Content notice

This game is about serious illness, terminal prognosis, and dying inside a bureaucratic system. It is deliberately unwinnable. The frustration is aimed entirely at the process — the rules, the queues, the incentives — and never at patients or at the fact of being ill. If that is not something you want to sit with right now, that is a completely reasonable choice. You can read the Sources or What Would Fix This pages instead, which contain no game.

Sources

Every obstacle in the game is drawn from a real regulation, guidance document, or published account. Nothing here is invented for effect. Where the game simplifies, the simplification is in the patient's favor — the real pathways are slower.

Expanded access requires the manufacturer's agreement
The FDA authorizes the large majority of expanded-access (compassionate use) requests it receives. But authorization is not supply: the company that makes an investigational drug must agree to provide it, and the FDA cannot compel a company to do so. A manufacturer may decline, and declining is not a violation. 21 CFR Part 312, Subpart I — Expanded Access to Investigational Drugs for Treatment Use. FDA, "Expanded Access" program guidance.
Single-patient IND still needs a supplier
An individual-patient expanded-access IND is real and can clear the FDA quickly. It authorizes a physician to administer the drug — but it does not produce the drug. Without a manufacturer willing to ship it, the paperwork closes the loop back on the previous obstacle. 21 CFR 312.310 — Individual patients, including for emergency use.
Right to Try changed the door, not the supply
The federal Right to Try Act removes the FDA from certain requests for eligible patients. It does not obligate a manufacturer to provide the therapy. The company still has to agree, and still does not have to. Right to Try Act of 2018, Public Law 115-176 (21 U.S.C. 360bbb-0a).
Eligibility scissors
Trials define who may enter through inclusion and exclusion criteria, and often through a performance-status measure (for example, ECOG or a disease-specific functional scale). Disease progression can move a patient across both lines at once: sick enough to meet the disease criteria, too unwell to meet the performance criteria. To the protocol this is not a contradiction. ICH E6(R2) Good Clinical Practice; ECOG performance status; individual trial protocols on ClinicalTrials.gov.
The control arm
Randomized controlled trials assign some enrolled patients to a placebo or to standard of care. Patients do not choose their arm and are typically not unblinded until the trial concludes. This design is how the field learns whether a therapy works; it also means enrolling can mean spending remaining time on the arm that is not the therapy. ICH E10 — Choice of Control Group in Clinical Trials; FDA guidance on placebo controls.
Insurance geometry
Federal law requires most plans to cover the routine patient costs of qualifying clinical trials. It does not cover the investigational item itself, and often does not cover travel, lodging, or every scan and complication that participation adds. The therapy can be free while being a subject is not. Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act §2709 (42 U.S.C. 300gg-8) — coverage of routine patient costs in approved clinical trials.
Institutional time versus patient time
Site IRB review, clinical-trial agreement negotiation, and clinical holds run on institutional calendars measured in weeks and months. A prognosis runs on the same units, and does not wait in the same queue. 21 CFR 312.42 — Clinical holds and requests for modification; 45 CFR 46 — IRB review.
Accelerated approval, then withdrawal
Accelerated approval lets the FDA approve a therapy for a serious condition on a surrogate endpoint reasonably likely to predict benefit, on the condition that a confirmatory trial verifies it. When the confirmatory trial reads out negative, the approval can be withdrawn — sometimes years after the surrogate answer, always after the people who needed the real answer needed it. 21 CFR Part 314, Subpart H; FDA Accelerated Approval Program; confirmatory-trial requirements strengthened by FDORA 2022.
Geography
A trial may enroll at one site, or a handful. For a patient far from all of them, participation means repeated travel on the protocol's schedule, for the protocol's duration — often for many weeks. Site listings on ClinicalTrials.gov; single- and limited-site enrollment in early-phase protocols.

What would fix this

These are real reform proposals, argued fairly, each with the strongest counterargument for why the current system is careful. The system is not cautious by accident; it is cautious because the alternatives have their own graves. Being fair about that makes the case land harder, not softer.

Publish expanded-access decisions and the reasons for them.

The counterargumentTransparency could deter small manufacturers with almost no supply from ever engaging, and could expose patient details. A company forced to explain every refusal in public may respond by never opening a program at all — which helps no one.

Ring-fence a share of investigational supply for expanded access, or fund the extra manufacturing.

The counterargumentInvestigational supply is genuinely scarce, especially for cell and gene therapies. Diverting doses to individual use can slow the pivotal trial — the trial that is the only path to the therapy reaching everyone. A dose given compassionately is sometimes a dose taken from the answer.

Make it clear that outcomes in single-patient use will not, on their own, sink a program.

The counterargumentThe FDA already reviews such data carefully rather than mechanically. And a safety signal is a safety signal: a rule that lets companies disregard bad outcomes in compassionate use would be dangerous to the next patient. The fear of the data is partly real and cannot simply be legislated away.

Use adaptive and response-adaptive trial designs to shrink or shorten placebo arms.

The counterargumentSmaller control arms and adaptive stopping can reduce statistical certainty and raise the odds of approving something that does not work — which produces its own, larger harm across everyone who then takes it. The placebo arm exists because false hope, at scale, also kills.

Cover the real cost of participation: travel, lodging, and trial-associated care.

The counterargumentThis is among the least contested proposals and is being piloted. But it costs money, and it does not fix the harder problems of supply or eligibility. It widens the door without moving the wall behind it.

Standardize and accelerate ethics review with a single reviewing IRB.

The counterargumentCentral review already exists for many federally funded multisite studies. Local review can catch local context a distant board misses, and speed traded for oversight is exactly the trade that produced the abuses IRBs were built to prevent.

Enforce confirmatory-trial deadlines for accelerated approval, with withdrawal when they lapse.

The counterargumentRecent law (FDORA, 2022) already tightened this. But withdrawing a therapy that some patients believe is helping them is wrenching, and surrogate endpoints are sometimes right. Faster withdrawal protects future patients at a visible cost to present ones — which is the same shape as the problem it is trying to solve.

None of these is free, and none of them is nothing. The honest position is that the system is a set of trades between the patient in front of you and the patients you cannot see yet. This game is about the ones in front of you, because they are the ones the trades are usually made against.
