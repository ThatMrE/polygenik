The literature

Does It Work

The following summarizes published research on open-label placebo — the general finding that people can respond to an inert pill even when they are told, plainly, that it is inert. These are third-party research findings about the placebo effect in general. They are not claims about this product, and this product has not been studied.

Irritable bowel syndrome (the foundational trial)

In a randomized controlled trial, 80 patients with IBS were assigned either to an openly labeled placebo pill (described to them as "placebo pills made of an inert substance") or to a no-treatment control. After three weeks, 59% of the open-label placebo group reported adequate symptom relief, compared with 35% of the no-treatment group — a difference the authors described as clinically meaningful.

Kaptchuk TJ, Friedlander E, Kelley JM, et al. "Placebos without Deception: A Randomized Controlled Trial in Irritable Bowel Syndrome." PLoS ONE, 2010; 5(12):e15591.

Chronic low back pain

In a later trial, 97 adults with chronic low back pain were randomized to their usual treatment alone, or to usual treatment plus an openly labeled placebo. After three weeks, the open-label placebo group reported roughly a 30% reduction in pain intensity and in pain-related disability, while the usual-treatment-only group showed little change.

Carvalho C, Caetano JM, Cunha L, et al. "Open-label placebo treatment in chronic low back pain: a randomized controlled trial." PAIN, 2016; 157(12):2766–2772.

Cancer-related fatigue

Among 74 cancer survivors with persistent fatigue, those given an openly labeled placebo for three weeks reported meaningfully greater improvement in fatigue and in fatigue-related quality of life than those receiving usual care, with the improvement partially sustained three weeks after the pills stopped.

Hoenemeyer TW, Kaptchuk TJ, Mehta TS, Fontaine KR. "Open-Label Placebo Treatment for Cancer-Related Fatigue: A Randomized-Controlled Clinical Trial." Scientific Reports, 2018; 8:2784.

Allergic rhinitis (seasonal allergy symptoms)

In a randomized study of patients with seasonal allergic rhinitis, an openly labeled placebo taken over two weeks was associated with a modest but measurable improvement in self-reported symptom burden relative to no additional treatment.

Schaefer M, Sahin T, Berstecher B. "Why do open-label placebos work? A randomized controlled trial of an open-label placebo induction with and without extended information about the placebo effect in allergic rhinitis." PLoS ONE, 2018; 13(3):e0192758.

Read the numbers carefully. These are small trials of specific conditions, mostly measuring self-reported symptoms over a few weeks. "Works about a third of the time" is a loose paraphrase, not a promise. The honest summary is: an inert pill, given openly, produced a real and measurable average effect in these studies — smaller than an effective drug, larger than nothing, and not universal. What that would mean for any individual person, taking this particular tablet, for any particular reason, is unknown and unstudied.

1. What this product is

Honest Placebo is an inert tablet. Its only ingredient is microcrystalline cellulose, a purified plant fiber used as a filler in ordinary tablets. It contains no drug and no active substance of any kind. It is sold as an object of interest and honesty, not as a health product.

2. What it is not

It is not a medicine, a supplement, or a treatment. It is not approved by the FDA or any other authority. It makes no claim to treat, cure, prevent, or diagnose any condition. It should never be used in place of medical care.

3. Before you take it

Do not use this product to manage symptoms, to replace a prescribed medicine, or to delay seeking care. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are giving anything to a child, speak with a qualified clinician first. When in doubt, do not take it.

4. About the placebo research

Published studies report that some people improve on an inert pill even when told it is inert (see, among others, Kaptchuk et al., 2010, in irritable bowel syndrome, with later work in chronic low back pain, cancer-related fatigue, and allergic rhinitis). These are general research findings about the placebo effect. They are not statements about this product, which has not itself been tested.

5. How it is taken

There is no dose and no schedule, because there is nothing to dose. Any tablet contains 300 mg of cellulose and nothing more. This leaflet does not recommend taking it for any purpose.

6. Possible effects

As an inert tablet, no pharmacological effect is expected. Microcrystalline cellulose is generally regarded as safe and is passed largely undigested. Rarely, any tablet may cause mild digestive discomfort. Stop use and seek advice if you feel unwell. As with anything swallowed, do not exceed comfort and do not give to anyone at risk of choking.

7. Storage

Store below 25°C, dry, in the original blister. Keep out of the sight and reach of children. Do not use after the expiry date printed on the pack. Dispose of responsibly.

The purpose of this leaflet is to tell you exactly what you are holding: nothing, described accurately. If any part of this document ever implies a health benefit from this product, that implication is unintended and incorrect.
