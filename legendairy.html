02

The Method

A skin swab is non-invasive and requires no clinician. We culture bacteria, not cells, which sidesteps every regulatory question the tissue would have raised. What comes out is a sculpture that happens to smell.

i.

Swab

A sterile cotton swab is drawn across a named, consenting site — a forearm, a cheek, the skin beside a glucose monitor. Nothing is broken, nothing is taken that the donor will miss.

ii.

Isolate

The swab is streaked onto agar and the resident flora is grown out, identified, and screened. Anything we cannot name is discarded. Nothing pathogenic reaches a wheel.

iii.

Inoculate

The identified isolate is introduced to warm milk alongside a conventional starter. The donor's own bacteria steer the flavor; the milk does the rest.

iv.

Age

Ten to sixteen weeks in a controlled cave. The rind sets, the paste develops, and a cheesemonger is brought in to taste it and write down the truth.

These objects are not food. They are cultured, aged, exhibited in vitrines, and never served. Not for human consumption. This is not a figure of speech.

04

The Lineage

This work does not pretend to be the first of its kind. It follows a specific precedent, and names it:

Selfmade (2013) — Christina Agapakis & Sissel Tolaas.

Christina Agapakis, a synthetic biologist, and Sissel Tolaas, a smell researcher, cultured a series of cheeses from bacteria swabbed off named people, exhibited them, and pointedly did not serve them. Nobody was sued, and the work is still cited. We followed that model exactly, because that is why it worked.

We do not launder the precedent. Crediting it is not a footnote here; it is part of the piece. Our only claim to a contribution is donor selection: where Selfmade used chefs and artists, LEGENDAIRY cultures the flora of the people who sell their own biology back to you — and puts it on a cheeseboard.

If you have not seen the original, seek it out first. Reading the room is the whole brand.
