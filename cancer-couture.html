The Premise

Yours by right.

 

The thing they took out of you was part of you. Convention calls it waste and hands it to disposal. We disagree. It is the most personal object you will ever own, and it belongs to no one else.

Each commission is fixed, sealed and made permanent. The specimen is stabilised, encased in optical resin and set in a housing that will outlast you. Nothing degrades. Nothing leaks. It is finished the way a stone is finished.

Every piece is made by hand in San Francisco, one at a time, by the people who will sign its certificate. We take a small number of commissions. We take them seriously.

How It Works

Four steps.

The path from pathology to finished piece is short, and we walk each part of it with you.

  1. Request your specimen

    After your diagnostic work is complete, ask pathology to release the fixed specimen to you. Most hospitals will, and it is your right to ask. We provide a release-letter template below.

  2. We send the kit

    Once you join the commission list, we send a sealed, pre-addressed fixation and shipping kit, with plain instructions and everything needed to return the specimen safely.

  3. You ship it back

    Seal the kit and post it to the studio. We confirm safe arrival the same day it reaches us, and confirm the setting you have chosen.

  4. You receive it in ten to fourteen weeks

    We complete the piece by hand and return it with its numbered certificate of provenance. Lead times vary by setting; each is listed above.

What We Cannot Accept

Some things we will decline.

Plainly, and without exception. These rules protect your care first, and the work second.

  • No specimen that is still needed for diagnosis. Diagnostic work must be fully complete, and we ask for a copy of the final pathology report before intake.
  • No unfixed tissue. We accept only specimens that have been properly fixed and stabilised. Fresh tissue cannot be posted and will be refused.
  • No specimen from anyone but the person it came from. The commission may be placed only by the patient, who has personally consented. However kindly meant, a relative cannot consent on another living person's behalf.
